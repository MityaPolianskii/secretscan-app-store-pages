Your iPhone keeps one list of apps that have asked to use Photos. Review that list, remove access you no longer need, and use App Privacy Report when you want evidence about recent access.

Five-minute permission audit

Check which apps requested Photos access

  1. Open Settings on your iPhone.
  2. Tap Privacy & Security, then tap Photos.
  3. Review every app in the list. Apple says an app does not appear here until it has asked for permission.
  4. Open each app entry and decide whether its current access is still needed for a feature you use.
  5. Reduce or remove access that is no longer justified, then verify that the apps you rely on still work as expected.

How to decide what an app needs

Start with purpose, not familiarity. A photo editor may need access while you are editing. A marketplace app may need one picture for a listing. A messaging app can often use Apple's photo picker for a deliberate selection. An app you no longer use usually has no continuing reason to browse your library.

  • Keep only purposeful access. You should be able to name the feature that needs it.
  • Prefer the narrowest useful option. If iPhone offers a more limited choice that still supports the task, use it.
  • Test after changing it. Reopen the app and confirm the feature you need still behaves correctly.
  • Revisit the list. New apps appear only after requesting permission, so the audit changes as your app library changes.

What changing a Photos permission does not do

A permission change controls how that app can access Photos on this device. It does not retract a picture you already sent, delete a copy created elsewhere, close a shared album, change a third-party account's privacy settings, or revoke a password or token visible in an image.

If a sensitive screenshot may already have left your control, use the screenshot exposure check to prioritize containment. If it contains a live credential, rotate or revoke that credential through the service that issued it.

Use App Privacy Report for recent activity

The Photos permission list shows what an app is allowed to access. App Privacy Report answers a different question: how apps have recently used privacy-sensitive data and device sensors, plus their network activity.

  1. Go to Settings, Privacy & Security, then App Privacy Report.
  2. Turn the report on if it is not already enabled.
  3. Use your iPhone normally, then return to the report and inspect the apps and data types listed.

Apple says the report starts collecting only after you enable it, stores its data on the device, and covers the previous seven days. Absence from a newly enabled report is therefore not proof that an app never accessed Photos.

When Safety Check is the better tool

If your concern involves personal safety, unwanted sharing with another person, an unknown device on your Apple Account, or permissions changed by someone else, open Settings, Privacy & Security, then Safety Check. Manage Sharing & Access supports a guided review. Emergency Reset is intentionally broad and should be used with awareness of the safety and communication effects.

What SecretScan does and does not do

SecretScan checks visible text in screenshots and photos using on-device OCR and deterministic pattern detection. It helps you find likely passwords, recovery codes, API keys, payment details, IDs, and similar text for your own review. It does not manage another app's Photos permission, prove that an app copied a photo, or erase an image from a third-party service.
